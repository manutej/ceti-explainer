# Goodhart's law · film id `goodhart` · explorer brief

## The exec question (one line)
"We set a target of 8 products per customer and the team is hitting it. Are we getting what the 8 stood for?"

## The belief to break
"If the number went up, the thing it measures went up." Once a number is the target, people can move the
number without moving the thing, and the dashboard cannot tell the difference: a manufactured mark and a real
one look identical until someone audits. (Goodhart 1975; in Strathern's 1997 wording, "when a measure becomes
a target, it ceases to be a good measure".)

## The one real case: Wells Fargo's cross-sell target, 2002 to 2016
| what | number | source |
|---|---|---|
| The target | 8 products per retail banking household ("Gr-eight", "Eight is great") | WF 10-Q Q1 2015; WF annual reports 1999, 2010 |
| What the metric reported | 6.13 products per household, Feb 2015 (6.17 a year earlier); 1 household in 4 had 8 or more | WF 10-Q Q1 2015 |
| 2016 consent order | 1,534,280 deposit accounts + 565,443 credit-card applications that may not have been authorised (about 2.1 M), May 2011 to mid-2015 | CFPB 2016-CFPB-0015 |
| Fired | about 5,300 employees, 1 Jan 2011 to 7 Mar 2016, for sales-practice violations | CFPB order; Board report 2017 |
| 2016 penalties | $185 M = CFPB $100 M + OCC $35 M + Los Angeles $50 M | CFPB order; WF 10-Q Q3 2016 |
| Expanded review (Aug 2017) | 3.5 M potentially unauthorised accounts (2.55 M + 0.981 M) among 165 M+ retail accounts opened Jan 2009 to Sep 2016 | WF 8-K Ex. 99.1, 31 Aug 2017 |
| 2020 settlement | $3 B to DOJ and SEC (incl. $500 M SEC fair fund); conduct 2002 to 2016 | DOJ OPA release, 21 Feb 2020 |
| The Goodhart sentence | "the publicly reported cross-sell metric included significant numbers of such unused or unauthorized accounts" | DOJ Statement of Facts (DPA Exh. A), 2020 |

Corrections to the seed: (1) the 3.5 M figure comes from Wells Fargo's own announcement of an expanded
third-party review (31 Aug 2017), not from the independent directors' report (10 Apr 2017); the directors'
report is the source for root cause and the 5,300 terminations timeline. (2) 565,443 are credit-card
*applications*, not issued cards. (3) Wells Fargo never reached 8 on average (6.13 in Feb 2015), so the film
must not say the bank "hit it"; the hook's "hitting it" belongs to the viewer's own team, not to Wells.

## The count structure
- **What is counted:** retail accounts opened, Jan 2009 to Sep 2016, scaled 165 M to 1,000 marks
  (1 mark = 165,000 accounts). A 40 × 25 grid of identical ink squares: the metric's view.
- **The truth on it:** the review flagged 3.531 M of 165 M, which is 21.4 per 1,000, so **21 marks** turn red
  (seeded positions). They were counted by the metric like the other 979.
- **Against the viewer:** the committed guess *g* (0 to 1,000; film default 100) is drawn as a graphite outline
  around the first *g* slots after the 21 red marks gather into slots 0 to 20, so guess and truth sit on the
  same grid at the same scale. Only then the ratio "21 ÷ 1,000 ≈ 2 in 100".
- **The turn:** 21 is a floor. Unused products sold with consent were in the metric too and are not flagged:
  the number broke without most people cheating.

## The Monday question
"What could move this number with nothing real behind it, and who would notice?"

## Honest-limits line
"3.5 M is the bank's review estimate (it may include some authorised accounts and misses unwanted products
sold with consent); one bank and one metric, not proof that every target goes bad."

## What this film is NOT
- Not "all metrics are bad" or "never set targets". It is about one target that people could satisfy
  without satisfying the customer.
- Not a fraud-detection or compliance film; no talk of controls, whistleblowers or audit procedure.
- Not about Wells Fargo's executives, clawbacks, CEO hearings or the 2018 asset cap.
- Not a claim that 3.5 M customers were harmed (accounts, not people; some may be authorised).
- Not a claim that Wells Fargo hit 8 (it reported 6.13).
- Not base rates, the planning fallacy, or the AI agent loop.
